Finding the two percent

Most complaints to a bank end with an apology and a form letter. About two in a hundred end with the bank paying money. I built a system that tries to spot those two before anyone has read them — from 4.8 million real complaints, on a student budget.

By Shivam BajajOctober 2026About 20 minutes
Try the live demo → Read the code

The short version

  • The data: 4.8 million real complaints that Americans sent to their consumer-finance regulator in 2022–2024. Nothing invented.
  • The idea: two readers, one score. A database works out each company's track record as it stood on the day a complaint arrived; an AI reads what the customer wrote; one model weighs both.
  • The result: on 150,000 complaints from 2024 it had never seen, reading the riskiest 10% catches 92.6% of the ones that end with the company paying.
  • The honest part: inside one company's own queue it puts a paying complaint above a non-paying one 82 times in 100 — and most of its misses come down to decisions the complaint never mentions.
01 — THE PROBLEM

Two in a hundred

In the United States, anyone with a problem with a bank, a lender or a credit bureau can file a complaint with the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau — the CFPB, a government agency. The CFPB forwards it to the company, which is expected to respond within 15 days, and then publishes the complaint in a public database.

Every complaint gets closed one way or another. The ending that matters most to the company is the rarest one: closed with monetary relief. The company paid — a refund, a reversed fee, some compensation.

So a complaints team faces the same question thousands of times a day: does this one need a senior analyst who reads every line, or will a standard reply do? Get it wrong one way and expensive people spend their week on form letters. Get it wrong the other way and a complaint that will cost money gets a template.

That's the first difficulty: it's a needle in a haystack. The second is that the needles aren't where the hay is.

An obvious shortcut is a rule: send every credit-card complaint to a senior. But only about one in seven of those pays, so most of that senior time is still wasted — and a rule ignores the rare expensive case elsewhere. Picture a student-loan complaint about a $200,000 loan that was never paid out. Student-loan complaints end in a payout about once in 90 times, so a product rule sends it a template. Only reading the actual words catches it.

Which complaints will cost money — and can a machine tell before a person has read them?

02 — THE DATA

Real people, real mess

My first design was wrong before it started. It imagined customers with histories — how much each had spent, how often they had complained before. The CFPB data has no customers at all: every complaint is anonymous. The only way to make that design work would have been to invent the customers, and invented data can't be checked by anyone. So the plan changed: track companies, and how each one handles each kind of problem.

The raw file is 9.2 GB — too big to open in one go — so it was read in slices of 250,000 rows. Before building anything on it, I wanted proof the complaints were written by people and not generated by a machine, because a language model trained on machine-made text would learn nothing real. The tests. How unevenly sentence lengths vary (people: 0.94; machine text is suspiciously regular, under 0.35) · how many different words appear in a fixed 100,000-word sample · how often texts open the same way · leftover template slots like {name}.

They passed, with one scare. The most repeated opening line looked like a template: “in accordance with the Fair Credit Reporting Act…”. It turned out to be legal wording that real people copy from credit-repair forums. Copy-paste, it turns out, is very human. One template appeared 30,110 times.

Those copies matter more than they look. Complaints with a unique text ended in a payout 3.84% of the time; texts that appear twice or more, 0.05% or less. They're mostly form letters disputing credit reports — and an exam full of them is an easy exam. So the final test never contains a text that also appears in the lessons. complaints with a story, from April 2022

One decision here shaped everything after it. About two thirds of the complaints have no written story, so the AI can never read them, and it was tempting to throw them away. But they hold 42% of all payouts. Without them every company's track record would be wrong — by a different amount for each company. So all 4.8 million stay: the AI reads 1.6 million, but the track records are built from every one. tech: features over the full population, training on a sample

03 — THE DATABASE

A database shaped like a snowflake

Everything lives in one database — PostgreSQL, on my laptop. One big table sits in the middle, one row per complaint; around it sit lookup tables for companies, products, problem types and states. Two of those branch again, like the arms of a snowflake. Explore it:

A few of the rules behind that shape sounded fussy and turned out to matter:

  • The answer is kept away from the clues. Whether a complaint paid lives in its timeline, not in the main table. To feed the answer to the model by accident, you'd have to go out of your way to fetch it.
  • Never assume a category means one thing. I assumed each sub-category belonged to a single category. It was false for 87.5% of complaints — the same sub-product name sits under several products. So a sub-category is identified by its name and its parent.
  • A blank isn't automatically missing. The "tags" column is empty for 94.5% of complaints and looks like junk. But empty means a box wasn't ticked: complaints from older Americans (62+) ended in a payout 10.08% of the time, against 1.06% for untagged ones.

The day the categories changed

In late August 2023 the CFPB changed its complaint form — renaming some products, splitting others, and renaming one of the most common problem types. Loaded naively, the database would have treated the new names as brand-new categories, and every affected company's history would have restarted from zero, weeks before my test period began. The renamed problem type alone covered 893,566 complaints.

The fix was a small translation table — old name in, today's name out — that re-labels 1,334,958 complaints by product and 337,252 by problem type. It wasn't a leak: both names are known the day a complaint arrives. It was a shift, and a shift gets fixed at the source. Doing it early took minutes; doing it after the models were built would have broken everything on top. tech: crosswalk table

The database also checks itself. Twelve deliberately wrong inserts — a mortgage sub-product filed under credit cards, for example — were all refused. One final query checks 21 facts about the load against numbers computed by a completely separate program, and prints PASS or FAIL for each. I fed it a wrong expected number to make sure it could fail.

04 — THE TRACK RECORD

No peeking at the future

The whole project rests on one rule: to score a complaint, use only what was known on the day it arrived. A model that sees even a sliver of the future looks brilliant in testing and fails in real life, like a student who had the answer key.

The track record asks a simple question — how often has this company paid on this kind of problem, before today? — and two details make it tricky. First, companies take time to answer: if a complaint arrived last week, its outcome isn't known yet. So the track record only counts complaints at least 60 days old. The CFPB never records when a company answered, so 60 days is an assumption — a cautious one, well past the 15-day deadline.

Second, many complaints share a date. On 43% of the days a company received complaints, it received more than one — once, 4,245 in a single day. The database's default way of looking back quietly includes everything from the same day, including the complaint being scored, so its own answer would leak into its own score. Every look-back therefore has an explicit rule. tech: window-function frames

Proving there's no peeking

Rules like this are easy to write and easy to get wrong, so I tested this one by trying to break it. I took one company's busiest day — 3,495 complaints — secretly flipped every answer (paid ↔ not paid), and recomputed its track record. For complaints on that same day and up to 59 days later, not one digit moved. On day 60 the payout rate jumped from 0.0034% to 0.34% — exactly when those outcomes are allowed to be known. Then the flip was undone. tech: leakage test in a rolled-back transaction

A test that can also fail is the only kind worth trusting.

And the track record is useful before any AI reads a word. Complaints whose company had paid out more than 15% of the time on that kind of problem really paid 28.74% of the time; where the company's rate was 1% or less, 0.03% did all complaints.

05 — THE CLUES

Small numbers lie

A company with three complaints, one of which paid, has a payout rate of 33%. It isn't a "33% payer". It's three complaints. Most of the 4,946 companies are small, and a model that takes tiny samples literally overreacts to noise.

The fix is one line. Before looking at a company, pretend it already has K imaginary complaints that paid at its product's usual rate — then add its real ones:

smoothed rate = (payouts + K × product's rate) ÷ (complaints + K)

A company with three complaints is mostly imaginary complaints, so it leans on its product's rate. A company with three thousand drowns the imaginary ones out. tech: shrinkage, empirical Bayes

Clues that are secretly clocks

Complaint volume grew fast: an average company's 90-day complaint count rose 5.5× from 2022 to 2024. A model trained on 2022–23 would see every 2024 company as unusually busy — it would be reading the calendar, not the complaint. Measuring each company's share of all complaints instead (up only 1.5×) fixes most of it, the way you'd compare a shop's market share rather than its raw sales in a growing economy.

The finished table stores 48 facts about every complaint. The model reads 19. The rest aren't wrong; they're clocks, counts that swell as time passes. To check, I trained a simple model on the 19, then on the 19 plus 16 of the left-out ones: 75.00 against 74.99. They added nothing. Choosing what not to feed a model is half the job. A one-setting speed-up. The first check of these counts ran for 11 minutes and wrote 28 GB of scratch files: by default, Postgres sorts in 4 MB of memory. Given 256 MB, the same check took 16 seconds.

06 — THE AI READER

Teaching a machine to read

The second reader is a language model called DistilBERT — a smaller, faster version of Google's BERT. Before it ever saw a complaint, it had learned English by reading enormous amounts of text and guessing hidden words. I then trained it further on one narrow question: given what this customer wrote, did the company end up paying? tech: fine-tuning a pre-trained transformer

It doesn't read words, exactly. It reads word-pieces from a vocabulary of about 30,000: common words are one piece, rarer ones get split — "chargeback" becomes charge + back. It can read at most 512 pieces, about 410 words, which covers 92% of complaints in full.

Then there's the CFPB's privacy tool. Before publishing, it replaces names, dates and account numbers with XXXX. Each XXXX costs the model two word-pieces; a hidden date costs six; across complaints, one piece in six is a blank. So before the AI reads anything, each run of blanks becomes a single marker — [REDACTED] or [DATE]. Not deleted: where the blanks fall is itself a clue.

I also tried the classic text clean-ups — on paper first. Reducing words to their dictionary form ("charged" → "charge") would, even done perfectly, have squeezed only 22% of the long complaints under the limit, while handing the AI unnatural sentences. Deleting common words would fit more, but the standard list of common words includes not, no and never: "I did not authorise this charge" becomes "authorise charge". Those tricks were made for programs that count words; a modern reader needs the not.

Training ran on a free Kaggle graphics card in about half an hour: three passes over 62,940 complaints, with the pre-trained part frozen for the first pass so the new layers could settle without scrambling what it already knew. One more trick mattered. The textbook speed-up — padding each batch only to its longest text — gained nothing here, because nearly every batch contains one very long complaint. Grouping similar lengths together cut the wasted work 2.23×.

07 — MEASUREMENT

Keeping score honestly

Here is how to build a model that is 97.7% accurate: answer "no payout" to every complaint. On 2024's complaints that's right 97.7% of the time — and completely useless. Accuracy lies about rare events, so I never used it.

The main score asks the question a triage desk actually has. Take one complaint that ended with a payout and one that didn't: how often does the model put the payout first? 50 is a coin flip; 100 is perfect. tech: ROC-AUC

Even that can flatter. Compare any two complaints and the model earns easy points just by knowing that credit-card complaints pay more than credit-report ones — true, and useless to a bank sorting its own queue. So every result is reported three ways:

The test is also arranged in time. The model learned from complaints up to September 2023, tuned its settings on October–December 2023, and sat its final exam on all of 2024. That matters because payouts keep getting rarer: 2.7% of complaints with a story paid in 2022, 1.7% in 2024. A random test would hide that; a test on the future can't. Sampling you can undo. With so few payouts, the model learned from every one of the 15,735 payouts in its lessons plus three non-payouts for each — 62,940 complaints, a quarter of them payouts. That inflates its guesses by a known amount, which is subtracted exactly afterwards. The tune and exam sets keep the real-world rarity.

And because the headline rests on 24 companies, every score comes with a range: redraw the test complaints 1,000 times and see where the score lands 95% of the time. The final model's 82.2 is really "somewhere between 81.5 and 83.0". Two models whose ranges overlap may differ only by luck. tech: bootstrap interval

08 — RESULTS

Is the database worth it?

The fair question about a project built around a database: would a good AI alone have done just as well? So I trained the pieces separately and together, and compared them on the same 2024 exam.

In the language a complaints manager uses: if seniors read only the riskiest 10% of complaints, the AI alone would put 88.8% of the paying ones in front of them; the AI plus the track record, 92.6%. On the 2024 exam that's about 130 more paying complaints found for the same amount of reading.

09 — LIMITS

Where it fails — and why a bigger model won't fix it

Scores are averages, so I read the model's worst mistakes one by one — the payouts it ranked lowest and the non-payouts it ranked highest, inside their own company.

The missed payouts were mostly complaints about credit reports or logging into an account — the kinds that almost never pay — where the company paid anyway, probably as a goodwill gesture. The false alarms read exactly like refund cases: a named fee, an amount, an unauthorised charge. And the company simply said no.

In both cases, the deciding fact lives inside the company, not in the complaint.

That's why a bigger AI can't simply push the score to 90: the information isn't in the text. It also made several tempting ideas easy to retire — after measuring them.

10 — CALIBRATION

Honest percentages

Ranking is what routing needs, but the live system also shows a number — "a 31.7% chance this complaint costs money" — and that number should mean something. A good weather forecaster is right about how often: on the days they say 30% rain, it rains about three days in ten.

Mine wasn't, at first. Over 2024 it predicted 3.1% of complaints would pay; 2.3% did. It had learned from 2022–23, when payouts were more common, and payouts kept falling. Two numbers fix it — one squeezes the model's over-confident extremes, one shifts everything down — fitted on January–June 2024 and checked on July–December. tech: Platt scaling

Why not tune the correction on the late-2023 practice data? Because payouts kept falling — October–December 2023 paid 3.50%, 2024 paid 2.31%. A correction tuned on 2023 would still forecast too high for 2024, which is exactly the problem being fixed. A fancier, free-form correction fitted slightly better, but it lumped complaints into ties and quietly lowered the ranking score. The simple one won.

Finally, the routing line: the riskiest 10% of complaints — a forecast of 3.67% or more — go to a senior analyst. In 2024, 21% of that group paid. Of everything else, 0.2%.

11 — DEPLOYMENT

Shipping it on a student budget

A model in a notebook is a science project. Putting it online meant solving three problems without breaking the rule that training and the live system see exactly the same numbers.

A scoreboard instead of a replay

Training needed each company's track record as it stood on the day of every past complaint; the database replays all 4.8 million to get that. A live complaint needs only today's record, in milliseconds. So the live system keeps a small scoreboard — counts of complaints and payouts per company and kind of problem — and a calculator that applies the same formulas.

Two ways of computing the same numbers is how deployed models quietly go wrong: if they disagree even slightly, the AI gets inputs it never learned from, and nothing raises an alarm. So I rebuilt the scoreboard as of three real past days — including the busiest day in the data — and compared all 19 inputs for 20,773 complaints with what training had used. Identical, to the last digit. tech: train/serve skew test Small enough to ship. The full database is 16 GB and stays on my laptop. Everything the live system needs fits in a 57 MB file.

Two sealed boxes

The trained model was exported to a single file that a small engine runs on an ordinary processor — no graphics card, no training software. On 1,000 real complaints it matched the original to five decimal places, in about 20 thousandths of a second each. The service and the model went into one sealed box, a small database into another, and one command starts both in the right order. tech: ONNX, Docker Compose

A rented computer and a receptionist

The boxes run on a small rented server in Sydney for about $21 a month, paid from free cloud credits. A receptionist program stands at the only open door. The demo site lives on Vercel: when you press "Score it", Vercel carries the request to Sydney and brings back the answer, so your browser only ever talks to one secure address.

Two things went wrong, both instructive. The cheapest cloud servers run at full speed only while they have "credits" saved up, and a new one starts with none — so my first speed test ran at a fifth of the server's speed. Credits refill whenever it's quiet, which for a demo is almost always; I kept the setting that can never cost extra. And when I locked the server so it would answer only Vercel, the secret I'd generated was 48 characters long — too long for the receptionist's default lookup table. It refused to start, and the site was down for about three minutes. My local test had used a short practice secret.

Real values break things that practice values don't.

12 — LESSONS

What I'd tell myself at the start

  1. Every number needs its group. "1.26%" means nothing until you say of what. Mixing figures from different groups forced a full round of corrections early on; after that, every number came from one generated file.
  2. Test on the future, and trust your lowest score. The flattering version of a result is usually measuring something easier than the job.
  3. Build the check that can fail. Flip the answers and see what moves. Feed the tester a wrong number. A check that can't fail proves nothing.
  4. Measure before you build. Half the data before eight times the data; the best case of a clean-up trick before writing it; K from a test, not a hunch.
  5. Choosing what not to feed a model is half the job. The clues that would have hurt most were the ones quietly encoding the calendar.
  6. One source of truth. The same SQL formulas and the same text clean-up feed training and the live system — and a test proves they agree.
  7. The ceiling is often information, not intelligence. When the deciding fact isn't in the data, a bigger model can't find it.
